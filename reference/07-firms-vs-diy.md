# Hiring a Firm vs. Doing It Yourself (§58c)

§58c citizenship is a **declaration** ("Anzeige"), not a discretionary application. You are *notifying* Austria of a legal entitlement, not petitioning for a favor. That legal shape is why Austria's own guidance says, in so many words, **"You do not need to engage a lawyer in order to file your declaration."** A lawyer is genuinely optional here — which makes "should I hire one?" a real cost/benefit question, not a given.

This file covers: (1) when hiring a firm actually pays off vs. when DIY is the right call, (2) how to vet a §58c firm so you don't overpay a generalist or a broker, (3) a shortlist of publicly-advertised §58c practitioners, and (4) a DIY success template — the sequence a self-filer follows, cross-checked against the official document requirements.

---

## 1. When to hire a firm vs. go DIY

The single biggest variable is **how much of your evidence you already hold vs. how much has to be dug out of Austrian archives.**

Two facts from the official guidance move the DIY line a long way in the self-filer's favor:

- **Austria researches its own archives for you.** Official wording: *"In case you do not have any documents concerning your persecuted ancestor, you do not have to ask for documentation to be searched and sent from Austria. The Austrian authorities will try to find evidence in archives."* The competent provincial authority (MA 35 in Vienna) will search for the ancestor's birth/citizenship/residence records itself. So the scariest-looking gap — "I don't have my grandmother's 1920 Vienna birth certificate" — is often **Austria's job, not yours.**
- **The declaration is free**, and for applicants born and living outside Austria, both federal and provincial fees are waived. You pay only for your *own-person* documents (birth certificate, criminal-record extract), their apostilles, any records you choose to order yourself, and translations if your documents aren't in German or English.

**Lean DIY when most of these hold:**
- Your descent chain is documentable with records you can order yourself (birth/marriage certificates in countries with functioning civil registries — US, UK, etc.).
- Your persecuted-ancestor evidence is either already in hand or is the kind Austria will pull (an Austrian birth/residence record).
- Your documents are in English or German (no sworn translation needed).
- You're comfortable with methodical archive correspondence and a months-long timeline.
- Eligibility is clean on its face (clear descent from a clearly-persecuted ancestor) — the hard part is *documentation*, not a legal judgment call.

**Lean toward hiring when:**
- The **anchor's persecution/flight is genuinely hard to evidence** and you want a professional to drive the Austrian-side archive research rather than wait on MA 35.
- **Documents need certified German translation** by a sworn translator (non-English/non-German originals) — a firm manages this cleanly.
- You're filing for a **whole family including minor children** and want one party to run the coordination, cross-referencing, and authority liaison.
- There's a **genuine legal wrinkle** — an ambiguous eligibility fact, a contested descent link, a name/identity discrepancy across documents you can't reconcile — where doctrinal judgment (not just legwork) earns its fee.
- You simply **value the hand-holding** and would rather pay to offload the whole thing.

**The honest middle:** many clean cases are DIY-able in a few months. What you're actually buying from a firm is (a) Austrian-side archive research you can't easily do from abroad, (b) certified translations, (c) apostille handling, and (d) someone who knows the "pitfalls in the details" and error-proofs the packet. If your documents are English-language and mostly in hand, a firm's value shrinks — and it's worth pricing that honestly before committing.

## 2. How to vet a §58c firm

No Austrian §58c attorney publishes prices — this is a **request-a-quote market**. That alone means you must do diligence before you can even compare. Run these checks:

**2a. Is it a registered Austrian attorney (Rechtsanwalt) — or a broker?**
This is the single most important check. A real Austrian attorney appears in a regional bar association (Rechtsanwaltskammer) directory — you can look them up by name and confirm an active (not suspended/inactive) registration, admission year, and seat. **Consultancies, "advisors," and citizenship-agencies are NOT Austrian attorneys.** Two of the biggest global brands in the citizenship space are investment-migration shops, not law firms — a poor fit for a *free statutory reparations entitlement* that has nothing to do with investment. If a provider frames §58c as a "program" you "invest" in, that's a mismatch.

**2b. Real §58c track record — or generic reviews?**
A firm can have hundreds of glowing reviews that are almost entirely about *unrelated* work (residence permits, company formation, ordinary disputes). Read the review *text*, not just the star count and total. You're looking for:
- **First-person §58c-descendant success stories** — someone describing the actual "descendant of Nazi-persecution victims" process and a successful outcome.
- **A concrete §58c figure the firm itself publishes** — e.g. "we have helped N clients file §58c declarations for descendants of Nazi persecutees." A specific case count is far stronger evidence than a large but generic review pile.
- **Doctrinal anchoring** — is the attorney a recognized citizenship-law specialist? The strongest signal is authorship of or contribution to the **standard legal commentary on the Austrian Citizenship Act (StbG)**. An attorney whose *core* practice is citizenship law is a different proposition from a general-litigation/business firm that has bolted on a §58c marketing page.

Watch for the gap between **marketing claims** and **independent corroboration**. "We've handled citizenship for all three generations of a Jewish family" appearing only on the firm's own website is a marketing claim; the same thing corroborated by a verifiable review, press coverage, or a published case count is evidence. Weight them differently.

**2c. Full-service scope — get it itemized.**
If you're paying to offload the work, confirm the quote actually covers all of it: (a) Austrian genealogical/archive research to nail the anchor-ancestor proof, (b) sourcing flight/persecution evidence, (c) procuring and assembling the descent-chain certificates, (d) certified German translations, (e) apostille handling, (f) filing for the **whole family including minors**, and (g) liaison with the deciding authority and consulate. Firms rarely itemize these publicly — make them do it in the quote.

**2d. Red flags**
- Not an Austrian-registered attorney (a broker/agency/"advisor").
- Investment-migration / citizenship-by-investment positioning applied to §58c.
- Pricing that's vague, gated, or tied to a "small evaluation fee" with no clear all-in number.
- §58c evidence that is *only* self-sourced marketing, with no independent corroboration and no reviews naming the actual descendant process.
- A bar-registry specialization that is business/litigation/real-estate with **no** citizenship listing — a soft flag that §58c is a practice add-on, not the doctrinal core. (Not disqualifying, but weigh it against firms whose core *is* citizenship.)

**2e. How to actually compare**
Because nobody publishes prices, the move is: shortlist two or three vetted firms, send each the ancestor's identifying details and your documents-in-hand list, and **request an all-in, flat-fee quote covering the whole family** (you + each child/minor), *including* archive research, translations, and apostilles — plus their estimated timeline and exactly how minors are filed. Then compare like-for-like. Do not pre-commit to one firm on the strength of a review count before you've priced at least one alternative.

### Free / low-cost first stop — IKG Vienna (Jewish Community of Vienna)
The **IKG Wien** holds Vienna Jewish community records (birth/marriage registers — *Matriken*), emigration and archive material. It is **not** a free descendant-help service — it's a **formal, fee-based archive request with a required signed user form.**
- **Channel:** email with the completed, signed user "DATA SHEET" attached. For a Vienna Jewish **birth register** record (*Matriken*), write the Records Office (Matrikenamt); for emigration/deportation/burial, the general Archive. Contact + forms: https://www.ikg-wien.at/en/archive
- **Fees:** written research is charged per started half-hour (roughly €50/half-hour for standard research; more for extensive family research), plus per-page certificate/scan fees — **owed even if the search finds nothing**, with no descendant/restitution discount.
- **Fee-exemption lever:** official requests from consulates/ministries/courts are fee-exempt — so the **cheapest path is to let the Austrian consulate or your hired firm pull the Vienna birth record** as part of the §58c process (which dovetails with "Austria researches its own archives"). Sending it yourself means paying the research fee for a parallel copy.
- **NB:** IKG will pull a *specific named record*; it does **not** build family trees or do open-ended genealogy for you.

## 4. DIY success template

Self-filing §58c is achievable — documented DIY cases have completed in roughly three months, after which a stack of records arrived from the Austrian archives. Here is the sequence a self-filer follows, cross-checked against the official document requirements.

**The core document requirements a self-filer must satisfy** (distilled from official guidance + DIY reports):
1. **Proof you are related to the persecuted ancestor** — the full descent chain (each birth/marriage certificate linking you → parent → … → ancestor).
2. The ancestor's **last known address inside modern-day Austria.** Addresses outside today's Austrian borders do *not* qualify. (Vienna is inside modern Austria; a former-Habsburg town now in another country would not.)
3. The **address the ancestor fled to**, plus **proof of the departure/flight date.**
4. The ancestor's **birth certificate** (Austria will often pull this itself).
5. Documents for **you and your parent(s)** in the chain.

**The self-filer sequence:**

1. **Establish eligibility on paper first.** Confirm your ancestor (a) held Austrian citizenship / a successor-state nationality / was stateless, (b) had their main residence in Austria before 15 May 1955, and (c) left because of Nazi persecution (or defense of the democratic Republic). Then confirm your unbroken direct-descent line to them. If both hold, you have a claim — the rest is documentation.

2. **Run the official online questionnaire for your consular district.** It generates your individualized document list *and* a pre-filled declaration form. (Filing at the wrong consulate is fatal — jurisdiction is your **main residence** and it's exclusive; see the jurisdiction reference.)

3. **Inventory: what you hold vs. what's a gap.** Map every required document to "have / can-order / Austria-pulls." Order the ones you can get yourself (your own birth certificate, the descent-chain certificates from civil registries).

4. **Assemble the persecuted-ancestor evidence you *can* get** — refugee/immigration records in the destination country, arrival/registration records, victim-recognition records. Don't panic over the Austrian-side gaps: the birth/citizenship/residence records inside Austria are what MA 35 will search for. The one gap DIY filers most often can't self-satisfy is the ancestor's **last registered Austrian address** — pursue it via the destination-country records that name it, or a Vienna residence-archive (Meldearchiv) request, but know it's also plausibly covered by Austria's own archive search.

5. **Apostille only what needs it.** Confirm the exact apostille scope with your consulate — it is often *much* narrower than feared. In the worked example, only each applicant's **own birth certificate and own criminal-record extract** needed apostilles; the ancestor and parent documents were accepted as **plain photocopies.** Don't over-apostille.

6. **Translations:** only for documents not already in German or English. English-language US/UK documents need no translation — a big DIY simplifier.

7. **File the declaration** at your correct consulate (mailing is typically acceptable; personal appearance is often not required, though your own-person civil documents must be submitted as apostilled originals). **Your retroactive citizenship-acquisition date is the date the authority (MA35) receives your declaration** (the consulate forwards it, so receipt can be somewhat later than the day you hand papers in), so there's value in filing sooner even with gaps — you can file *before* Austria has pulled the ancestor's Austrian birth record.

8. **Family coordination.** If several relatives file, **only one** needs to submit the ancestor-evidence packet; each other applicant files at *their own* consular district and attaches a brief note referencing the first filing. So "file as a family" means **coordinated cross-referencing across possibly several consulates**, not one joint filing at one office.

**Extra DIY leads worth knowing:** for a Jewish ancestor there is often a "mighty paper trail" that *exists but isn't digitized* — Austrian finding aids like **findbuch.at** (an Austrian archival finding-aid database that unlocks several victim/restitution databases) can surface it, and you can pay the Austrian archives to research an ancestor directly. Active applicant communities (e.g. the r/AustrianCitizenship subreddit and dedicated Facebook groups) are a good place to compare notes with people who've just done it.
